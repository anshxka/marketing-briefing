ET BrandEquityGreat Lakes Institute of Management

Great Lakes Report Warns Generic AI Content Threatens Brand Distinctiveness and Trust

As brands rapidly accelerate their adoption of generative tools, creative distinctiveness and authentic storytelling are emerging as critical casualties. A new report by the Great Lakes Institute of Management reveals that marketing leaders consider generic content to be the single biggest threat AI poses to brand storytelling, closely followed by the risk of losing consumer trust and an authentic brand voice.

Presented at Marketing Conclave 2026, the study surveyed more than 120 senior marketing and business leaders across 16 industries. It found that 42% of organisations are already AI-first by design or deploying AI at scale, with 80% expecting AI-generated brand content to surge over the next two to three years. Only two respondents reported having never explored AI in marketing. However, while AI's promise in areas like CRM engagement remains largely untapped, its widespread deployment is stoking fears of creative sameness.

Addressing the conclave, Kanika Saxena, VP at Vodafone Idea, warned that homogenised messaging risks breaking customer connections. Citing breast cancer awareness campaigns that failed to resonate due to a lack of empathy and real-life context, she emphasised that while AI speeds up production, human perspective and context remain essential to prevent eroding trust.

Why it mattersScaling content volume via AI is counterproductive if it dilutes your brand's unique point of view into feed-filling sameness. Maintaining distinctiveness requires human insight, empathy, and real-life context that automated generation tools cannot replicate on their own.

Marketing DiveConsumer behaviour & trends

Why Marketers Must Treat Gen Alpha as Current Household Decision-Makers

Marketers at Advertising Week New York highlighted the rising economic weight of Gen Alpha, a cohort of approximately 2 billion consumers globally forecast to reach a valuation of $5.46 trillion by 2029. Rather than treating this demographic—typically defined as consumers aged 14 to 16 and younger—as a long-term prospect, brands are being urged to recognize their immediate commerce influence. Findings from DKC indicate that Gen Alpha already commands more than $100 billion in direct spending power and shapes 42% of household purchases.

Industry leaders noted that this outsized influence requires a shift in how marketing budgets are allocated across generations. Emma Witkowski, vice president of media solutions at WildBrain, stated during an Advertising Week panel that Gen Alpha has effectively become the "household CMO." She warned that brands deferring engagement until these consumers become adults are passing up both short-term spending and long-term brand equity.

At the same time, brands must carefully navigate how young consumers perceive their own identity in relation to modern technology. Research from GWI and Razorfish shows that over one-fifth of Gen Alpha engages with artificial intelligence every day. However, Donna Tuttle, vice president of marketing and communications at Whataburger, emphasized on a Razorfish-moderated panel that Gen Alpha and Gen Z actively reject being pigeonholed as the "AI generation," feeling irritated when brands define them strictly through the tools they use.

Why it mattersMarketers must account for Gen Alpha's immediate sway over household wallets today instead of delaying targeting until they become independent spenders. When engaging this cohort, brands should seamlessly integrate digital-first experiences while avoiding superficial generational clichés like branding them solely around AI.
