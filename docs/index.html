TechCrunch AIBlock

Block Unveils Dessa, a Humanlike Full-Duplex Voice AI Model That Runs Locally

Jack Dorsey’s Block has unveiled Dessa, a new full-duplex voice model developed by its research lab that mimics human speech nuances such as laughter, pauses, and emotional inflections. Unlike conventional voice systems that convert speech to text and back, Dessa is trained entirely on human conversational speech and can speak and listen simultaneously. It operates without relying on an intermediate text pipeline to steer emotional expression and was trained using a single Nvidia GPU rack.

Demonstrations released by the company show the AI expressing fatigue, conveying vulnerability, and interacting casually during games. Block designed Dessa to be lightweight enough to run locally on consumer-grade hardware, specifically a MacBook M4 Max or higher, eliminating the latency and cloud compute expenses tied to typical voice pipelines.

Currently, Dessa is not accessible to the public as Block has invited safety researchers to evaluate risks, particularly around deceptive conversational scenarios. For Block, which supports small businesses through products like Square's automated voice ordering, deploying an efficient, on-device voice agent could substantially cut operational costs while delivering more natural customer interactions.

Why it mattersAs voice models gain humanlike emotional range and operate directly on-device, brands will be able to deliver hyper-realistic conversational experiences at significantly reduced infrastructure costs. However, marketers will need to tread carefully with consumer trust and disclosure as automated interactions become nearly indistinguishable from real people.

The Verge AIRegulation & policy

Pennsylvania Enacts Law Requiring Disclosures on AI-Generated Advertisements

Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro has signed the Artificial Intelligence Deceptive Advertising Act into law, making the state one of the first in the US to mandate disclosures on AI-generated commercial content. The legislation, introduced by State Senator Nick Pisciottano, requires any advertisement—across video, audio, text, or still images—that has been "significantly modified or generated by artificial intelligence" to display a label in the same format as the ad itself. The regulation applies equally to high-budget television commercials and local print promotions.

Because the mandate applies to any ad running within Pennsylvania regardless of where it was produced, campaigns airing across multiple states may begin showing disclosures more broadly. Advertisers broadcasting regional or national media buys will need to account for Pennsylvania's presence in their distribution footprints.

However, the legislation contains several notable ambiguities. The statute provides carve-outs for parody, satire, commentary, criticism, and material deemed "evidently fictional," though the definition of "evidently fictional" is not clarified. Additionally, the language indicates disclosures are primarily triggered when synthetic content creates a "false consumer perception" about a product or service, leaving legal uncertainty over how falsity and significant modification will be interpreted.

Why it mattersThis law signals an accelerating shift toward mandatory labeling for synthetic media, requiring creative teams to rethink the disclosure protocols on AI-enhanced assets. Marketers running multi-market campaigns must audit their generative assets to avoid compliance risks in jurisdictions with stricter advertising transparency laws.
